# What you only learn after living in Fukuoka: how important are delivery lockers for online shoppers?

Shopping online in Japan is a real pleasure. Prices are transparent, items are delivered to your door, and they usually arrive the next day.

But there is one reality that many people don't think about before moving to Fukuoka:

**If you're not home that day, the courier won't ask a neighbour to sign for your parcel, and won't leave it at the door either.**

A small missed-delivery slip (fuzai-hyō, 不在票, also called fuzai renraku-hyō, 不在連絡票) is left in your letterbox, and the parcel is taken away again. Next, you need to arrange a redelivery (saihaitatsu, 再配達).

It doesn't sound difficult, but the process is more of a hassle than you might think.

Today let's talk about delivery lockers (takuhai box, 宅配ボックス) — and what your online shopping life in Fukuoka looks like without them.

### 1. What are delivery lockers?

Delivery lockers are smart parcel lockers installed at the entrance of an apartment building. The courier locks the parcel straight into a compartment, and when you get home you collect it with a code or a key — no waiting for anyone and no need to fit around delivery times.

In Taiwan or Hong Kong, many buildings have a caretaker who receives parcels for you. But in Fukuoka this is almost unheard of. Most apartment buildings have no resident caretaker, so when a parcel arrives, you're the only one who can receive it.

### 2. Without delivery lockers, this is the reality

You get home to find a missed-delivery slip in your letterbox. It has a QR code or a phone number, asking you to book a new delivery time.

The process itself isn't too hard — scan the QR code, choose a time slot, confirm. But if your Japanese isn't quite there yet, even understanding the wording on the slip takes a while. And if you have to phone, the pressure is even greater.

What's more, even if you successfully book a slot, the parcel won't necessarily arrive on time.

I once booked a delivery for the 8am–12pm slot, and it didn't arrive until 12:30. Those hours waiting at home for a parcel were, honestly, simply wasted.

### 3. How hard is redelivery if your Japanese isn't fluent yet?

This is something I especially want to say to international students and new residents who have just arrived in Fukuoka.

The barriers to redelivery are higher than you'd think:

- The instructions on the missed-delivery slip are entirely in Japanese, and you may not understand them at a glance the first time
- Some delivery companies require a phone call, which can be quite stressful if your Japanese isn't fluent
- The QR code booking page is also entirely in Japanese

The three main delivery companies (Yamato Transport, Sagawa Express and Japan Post) each have different redelivery procedures. And there is usually a time limit — about a week or so; after that the parcel is returned to the sender, and sorting it out becomes even more troublesome.

### 4. Without delivery lockers, are there other options?

Yes, but each one has its limits:

**①** **Convenience store pick-up**

Have the parcel sent to a nearby 7-Eleven, Lawson or FamilyMart and collect it there. Convenient, but not every product supports it, and bulky items can't be sent this way.

**②** **Leave-at-door delivery (okihai, 置き配)**

Ask for the parcel to be left outside your front door without a signature. Platforms such as Amazon support this, but it requires the property to let delivery staff reach your door — most apartment buildings in Fukuoka have strict entry control, so this option becomes pointless.

**③** **Delivery companies' own pick-up points**

For example, Yamato's smart pick-up stations, where you can collect parcels yourself. Suits people who don't like waiting at home.

Together, these options can cover most situations — but each time there's an extra step, and over time it starts to feel like a chore.

### 💡 Local insight: when choosing a home, delivery lockers deserve a place on your checklist

From an agent's point of view, whether a property has delivery lockers is something many clients tend to overlook when narrowing down their options.

A few things worth thinking through in advance:

- **New builds (shinchiku, 新築) almost always have them**, while older wooden low-rise apartment buildings (mokuzō apāto, 木造アパート) mostly don't — you can start filtering right there
- **Ask yourself how often you shop online**: buying one or two things now and then is fine, but if you're buying every week, the redelivery process will really wear you down
- **It's also worth checking whether leave-at-door delivery is possible**: some properties have strict entry control and delivery staff can't get into the lobby, so this option doesn't work at all

It's not that you can't rent a place without delivery lockers — but if you know this reality in advance, you'll have a clearer idea when choosing a home of whether you can live with it.
